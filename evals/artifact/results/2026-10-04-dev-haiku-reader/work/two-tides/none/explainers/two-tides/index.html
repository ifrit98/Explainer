Why two high tides? An interactive view

Companion to explanation.md. The Earth is shown from above the North Pole. The Moon is kept fixed on the right, so the Earth appears to turn once every 24 h 50 min, which is one lunar day.

Things to try
  1. Choose the first arrow option and set the distance to 60. The arrows all look almost identical, because the whole Earth is pulled almost uniformly. Now drag the distance down to 3. The near-side arrows get longer and the far-side arrows get shorter.
  2. Switch to "minus pull on the centre". Arrows point out toward the Moon, out away from the Moon, and in at the sides. That pattern is what makes the two bulges. It looks the same at every distance, and only its strength changes.
  3. Press Play and watch the white dot ("you"). It passes under a bulge twice each lunar day, and the graph shows two peaks.
  4. Turn on the Sun. Set days to 0 (new moon) or about 14.8 (full moon) for spring tides, or about 7.4 or 22 (quarter moons) for neap tides.

The water bulge is exaggerated about 100,000 times. The heights are for an idealised ocean covering the whole planet (the "equilibrium tide"), not for any real coast. Arrows show the Moon's effect only.
