# How does a vaccine prepare the immune system for a disease it has never met?

<!-- Stage 1, rendered from model.md in STE-80. -->

**The immune system does not design a defence after it sees a disease. Before any infection, it already holds billions of cells with random receptor shapes. A vaccine shows it a harmless piece of the pathogen, the rare matching cells multiply, and some stay as memory cells. When the real pathogen arrives, the response starts from a large, ready population instead of a few cells.**

## The parts

- An **antigen** is a molecular shape on a pathogen, for example a protein on the surface of a virus.
- A **lymphocyte** is a white blood cell (a B cell or a T cell). Each lymphocyte carries one **receptor** type, a protein that binds one shape.
- An **antibody** is a free-floating copy of a B cell receptor. It binds the antigen and blocks the pathogen or marks it for destruction.

## How it works

<!-- claim: selection-not-design -->
1. **Random receptors, made in advance.** Each new lymphocyte shuffles gene segments at random to build its receptor. The number of possible receptors is more than 10^11. The set of receptors in your body is your **repertoire**. It already contains a few cells that fit almost any shape, including shapes from viruses that did not exist when you were born.
2. **The antigen selects the match.** For a given antigen, only about 1 in 100,000 to 1 in 1,000,000 lymphocytes has a receptor that binds it. The vaccine brings the antigen to the lymph nodes, where those rare cells meet it.
3. **Clonal expansion.** A lymphocyte that binds the antigen, and also gets an alarm signal, starts to divide. Over about a week, it makes a clone of thousands of identical cells. B cells in the clone make antibodies.
4. **Memory cells remain.** After the response, most clone cells die. Some become **memory cells**: long-lived lymphocytes with the matching receptor. There are now about 10–100 times more matching cells than before. Many have a better fit, because the clone mutates its receptors during expansion and the best binders are kept.

Analogy: the repertoire is a large ring of random keys. The vaccine does not cut a new key. It finds the keys that fit this lock and copies them. (Unlike keys, the copies can also improve their fit.)

A vaccine supplies the antigen without the disease: an inactivated pathogen, one purified protein, or mRNA that tells your cells to make that one protein briefly.

## Why it works: a race

<!-- claim: why-harmless-antigen -->
<!-- claim: memory-faster -->
Infection is a race between pathogen growth and immune expansion. Assume, for illustration, a pathogen that multiplies 10 times per day.

| | First meeting (no vaccine) | Second meeting (after vaccine) |
|---|---|---|
| Matching cells at the start | a few rare naive cells | 10–100 times more memory cells |
| Useful antibody after | 7–14 days | 2–4 days |
| Antibody level | baseline | 10–100 times higher |
| Pathogen growth before response (10×/day) | 7 days: 10,000,000-fold | 3 days: 1,000-fold |

The vaccine moves the slow first response to a safe time. The real infection then meets the fast second response. The pathogen can still enter the body, but the immune system stops it while it is small, so you get no illness or a mild one.

All day counts and frequencies are typical estimates; they vary by antigen, vaccine, and person.

## Limits

- **Changed antigen.** Memory receptors fit the old shape. A variant with a changed antigen (as with influenza each year) binds less well, so protection drops and the vaccine needs an update.
- **Decline over time.** Antibody levels fall, and for some vaccines memory also weakens. Boosters restore them (tetanus: about every 10 years).
- **Weak immune system.** A person who makes few lymphocytes (for example, during chemotherapy) cannot expand a clone well, so the vaccine protects less.
