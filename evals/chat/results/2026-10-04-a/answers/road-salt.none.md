# Why does salt melt ice on roads?

_none_

Salt melts ice because it **lowers the freezing point of water**, an effect called **freezing point depression**.

**What happens**

1. **Ice always has a thin layer of liquid water on it.** Even below 0°C (32°F), molecules at the surface constantly switch between solid and liquid.

2. **Salt dissolves into that liquid layer.** Sodium chloride (NaCl) breaks apart into sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) ions.

3. **The ions get in the way of freezing.** For water to freeze, its molecules must lock into an orderly crystal lattice. Dissolved ions crowd in among the water molecules, so fewer of them reach the ice surface and attach. Meanwhile, molecules keep leaving the ice at the same rate.

4. **Melting outpaces freezing.** With more molecules leaving the ice than joining it, the ice melts. That creates more salty water, which dissolves more salt and melts more ice.

**Why it works this way**

Freezing point depression is a **colligative property**: it depends on the *number* of dissolved particles, not on what they are. That's why:
- NaCl splits into 2 ions per unit, making it more effective than a substance that doesn't dissociate, like sugar.
- **Calcium chloride (CaCl₂)** splits into 3 ions and also releases heat as it dissolves, so it works better in very cold conditions.

**The limits**

- Rock salt works well down to about **-9°C (15°F)**. Below that, it melts ice too slowly to be practical.
- The theoretical minimum for saltwater is about **-21°C (-6°F)**. Below that, even saturated brine freezes.
- In extreme cold, road crews switch to calcium chloride or magnesium chloride, or use sand for traction instead.

**Downsides**

Road salt corrodes cars and bridges, damages concrete, and runs off into soil and waterways, where it can harm plants and aquatic life. Some areas use alternatives like beet juice brine mixtures to reduce these effects.
