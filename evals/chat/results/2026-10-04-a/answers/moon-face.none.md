# Why do we always see the same side of the Moon?

_none_

We always see the same side of the Moon because of **tidal locking**, also called synchronous rotation.

## The short answer
The Moon spins on its axis exactly once for every orbit it makes around Earth, about 27.3 days for each. Because the rotation and the orbit are synchronized, the same face always points toward us.

The Moon does rotate. If it didn't spin at all, we'd see all sides of it over the course of a month.

## How it got that way
1. **Earth's gravity stretched the Moon.** Gravity is stronger on the near side of the Moon than the far side, so the Moon gets pulled into a slightly elongated shape with a "tidal bulge" pointing roughly toward Earth.

2. **The early Moon spun faster.** Billions of years ago, the Moon rotated more quickly than it orbited. As it spun, its bulges were carried slightly out of line with Earth.

3. **Gravity tugged the bulge back.** Earth's pull on the misaligned bulge created a torque that acted like a brake, gradually slowing the Moon's spin. Internal friction from the constant flexing turned that rotational energy into heat.

4. **Equilibrium was reached.** The slowing continued until the rotation period matched the orbital period. At that point the bulge stays aligned with Earth, there's no more braking torque, and the Moon stays locked.

This likely happened relatively quickly in the Moon's history, within perhaps tens of millions of years.

## Some interesting details
- **We actually see about 59%, not 50%.** The Moon wobbles slightly from our point of view, an effect called *libration*. This happens because its orbit is elliptical (so its orbital speed varies while its spin stays constant), its orbit is tilted, and we view it from different points as Earth rotates.
- **The "dark side" isn't dark.** The far side gets just as much sunlight as the near side. It's only "dark" in the sense that it was unknown until the Soviet Luna 3 probe photographed it in 1959.
- **Tidal locking is common.** Most large moons in the solar system are tidally locked to their planets, and Pluto and its moon Charon are locked to *each other*.
- **Earth is slowly being affected too.** The Moon's tides are gradually slowing Earth's rotation, lengthening our day by roughly 2 milliseconds per century. The Moon is also drifting away from us at about 3.8 cm per year.
