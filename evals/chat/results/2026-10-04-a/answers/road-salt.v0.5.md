# Why does salt melt ice on roads?

_v0.5_

**Short answer:** Salt does not heat the ice. Salt lowers the temperature at which water freezes. On a road at −5 °C, pure water stays frozen, but salty water stays liquid. So the ice melts into brine (salt water).

## The mechanism

1. **Ice at 0 °C is in balance.** Water molecules leave the ice surface and enter the liquid. Other molecules leave the liquid and join the ice. At 0 °C, the two rates are equal, so the amount of ice does not change.
2. **Salt dissolves in the thin film of liquid water on the ice.** There is almost always some liquid, from sunlight, tire friction, or the surface itself. Each NaCl unit splits into two ions: Na⁺ and Cl⁻.
3. **The ions reduce the freezing rate.** The ions occupy space at the ice surface, so fewer water molecules reach the ice and attach to it. The ions do not enter the ice crystal.
4. **The melting rate does not change.** The ice itself is still pure water, so molecules leave it at the same rate as before.
5. **Melting now exceeds freezing.** The ice shrinks. To restore the balance, the temperature must drop below 0 °C. That lower temperature is the new freezing point.

**Important:** melting absorbs heat. The brine takes that heat from the road and the air, so the mixture gets *colder* than the ice was. Salt melts ice even though it removes heat.

## How much salt does

The effect depends on the **number of dissolved particles**, not on what the particles are (established fact; chemists call this a *colligative property*).

ΔT ≈ i × K × m

- *K* = 1.86 °C per mole of particles per kg of water
- *m* = moles of salt per kg of water
- *i* = particles per salt unit (2 for NaCl)

**Worked example:** dissolve 58 g of NaCl (1 mole) in 1 kg of water. Ideal estimate: 2 × 1.86 × 1 ≈ 3.7 °C lower. The measured value is about 3.4 °C, because ions in real brine do not act fully independently.

## Where it works and where it fails

| Road temperature | Result with rock salt (NaCl) |
|---|---|
| −5 °C | Works. Brine can stay liquid down to well below −5 °C. |
| about −10 °C | Works slowly. Road crews treat this as the practical limit. |
| −21 °C | Theoretical limit. No NaCl concentration keeps water liquid below this. |
| −30 °C | Fails. The ice stays solid, and crews use sand for grip. |

For colder roads, crews use calcium chloride (CaCl₂). It gives **3** ions per unit instead of 2, so it lowers the freezing point more. It also releases heat when it dissolves. It works to about −30 °C in practice.

## Scope

This answer leaves out salt damage to cars, concrete, and plants. It also leaves out the deeper thermodynamic reason, chemical potential, behind step 3. Look up "freezing-point depression" and "colligative properties" for the full derivation.
