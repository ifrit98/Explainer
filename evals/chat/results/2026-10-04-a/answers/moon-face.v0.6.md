# Why we always see the same side of the Moon

**Short answer:** The Moon does rotate. It turns once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to orbit Earth once, about 27.3 days. Because the two periods match, the same face always points at Earth. This match is called **tidal locking**. Earth's gravity caused it, and Earth's gravity keeps it.

## 1. Why matching periods hide one side

Picture walking in a circle around a chair while you keep your face toward it. After one lap, you have also turned once to face every wall of the room. You rotated once per orbit, so the chair only ever saw your face.

- **If the Moon did not rotate at all**, it would keep facing the same direction in space. From Earth, we would see every side over one orbit.
- **If the Moon rotated faster**, we would also see every side, just faster.

Only an exact 1:1 match keeps one face toward Earth.

## 2. How Earth forced the match

1. **Gravity stretches the Moon.** Gravity weakens with distance. Earth pulls harder on the Moon's near side than on its far side. This difference stretches the Moon slightly along the Earth–Moon line into two **tidal bulges**, one facing Earth and one facing away.
2. **Rotation carries the bulges off-line.** The early Moon probably rotated faster than it orbited. The rock cannot reshape instantly, so rotation dragged the bulges a little ahead of the Earth–Moon line.
3. **Earth pulls the bulges back.** Earth's gravity pulls the near bulge back toward the line. This creates a **torque**, a twisting force, that opposes the spin.
4. **Friction removes energy.** Each rotation re-stretches the rock, and internal friction converts spin energy to heat. The spin slows.
5. **Slowing stops at the match.** When the rotation period equals the orbital period, the bulges stay on the Earth–Moon line. Earth's pull then points straight through them, so the torque is zero. Nothing slows or speeds the spin further.

The locked state is also **stable**. If the Moon turned slightly too fast or too slow, the bulge would drift off the line, and Earth's pull would push it back.

**How long it took:** Models estimate tens of millions of years or less. That is short compared with the Moon's age of about 4.5 billion years. *(Estimate: it depends on the early Moon's interior and distance.)*

## 3. What "the same side" really means

We see about **59%** of the surface over time, not 50%. The Moon's orbit is slightly elliptical. Its orbital speed changes during each orbit, but its rotation speed stays constant. Its axis is also tilted relative to its orbit. These effects, called **libration**, make it appear to rock slightly, so we see a little past each edge. *(Observation.)*

## 4. The same process is acting on Earth

The Moon raises tides on Earth too, and those tides slow Earth's rotation.

- **Days get longer**, by about 2 milliseconds per century. *(Observation from ancient eclipse records and coral growth bands.)*
- **The Moon moves away**, by about 3.8 cm per year. Spin that Earth loses goes into the Moon's orbit. *(Observation from laser reflectors left on the Moon.)*

Earth is much more massive, so it locks far more slowly. Pluto and its moon Charon have finished this process. Each always shows the other the same face.

**Scope:** This answer leaves out the orbital mechanics of Earth–Moon recession and the debate about how the Moon formed.
