# Why salt melts ice on roads

**Short answer:** Salt does not heat the ice. Salt lowers the temperature at which water freezes. At −5 °C, pure water stays frozen, but salty water stays liquid. The ice melts because it is now above the freezing point of the salty water around it.

## The mechanism: two opposite flows at the ice surface

At the boundary between ice and liquid water, molecules move in both directions all the time:

- **Melting flow:** water molecules break free from the ice and join the liquid.
- **Freezing flow:** water molecules in the liquid attach to the ice.

At 0 °C, the two flows are equal for pure water. The amount of ice stays constant. This balance point is the **freezing point**.

Salt changes only one of the two flows:

1. Salt (NaCl) dissolves in the liquid film on the ice and splits into Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.
2. The ions take up positions in the liquid. Fewer water molecules are at the ice surface, so fewer can attach. **The freezing flow decreases.**
3. The ice is still pure water, so ions do not affect it. **The melting flow stays the same.**
4. Melting now exceeds freezing, so the ice shrinks.
5. Balance returns only at a lower temperature, where the melting flow is slow enough to match the reduced freezing flow. That lower temperature is the new freezing point.

(This is the molecular picture. The formal explanation uses entropy: dissolved particles make the liquid state more favourable. Both give the same result.)

## How much lower? A worked example

The drop depends on the **number of dissolved particles**, not on what the particles are. For dilute solutions (established fact, approximate):

**ΔT ≈ i × 1.86 °C × m**

- *m* = moles of dissolved substance per kg of water
- *i* = particles per unit (NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻, so *i* = 2)

Dissolve 58 g of salt (1 mol) in 1 kg of water: ΔT ≈ 2 × 1.86 × 1 ≈ 3.7 °C. The measured value is about 3.4 °C, because ions interact at this concentration. The water now freezes at about −3.4 °C.

More salt gives a bigger drop, until the water cannot dissolve more salt. Saturated brine freezes at about **−21 °C**. In practice, rock salt works poorly below about **−10 °C**, because it dissolves and acts too slowly.

## Why not some other substance?

Because only the particle count matters, sugar also lowers the freezing point. But a sugar molecule weighs about 342 g/mol and does not split, so it gives about 12 times fewer particles per gram than salt. Salt is cheap, very soluble, and gives two particles per unit.

Calcium chloride (CaCl₂) splits into three ions (*i* = 3) and releases heat as it dissolves. It works down to about −30 °C, so it is used in colder climates.

## Where does the melting energy come from?

Melting 1 g of ice needs about 334 J. Salt supplies none of it. The heat comes from the road, the air, and the ice-water mixture itself. That is why salty slush can be colder than 0 °C: it cooled itself by melting ice.

## Two practical consequences

- **Salt needs some liquid to start.** Usually a thin film of water is always present on ice, or sunlight and traffic make one. Dry salt on very cold, dry ice does little.
- **Salt has a temperature limit.** Below about −10 °C, road crews often use sand for grip instead, or switch to CaCl₂.
