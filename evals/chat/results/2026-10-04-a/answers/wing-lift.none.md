# How does an airplane wing make lift?

_none_

An airplane wing makes lift by **deflecting air downward**. By Newton's third law, pushing air down pushes the wing up. Pressure differences around the wing do the pushing.

## The core idea

A wing is shaped (cambered) and tilted slightly nose-up (its **angle of attack**) so that the air flowing past it gets turned downward. Behind a flying plane there's a large mass of air moving down, called **downwash**. Turning that air requires a force, and the equal and opposite reaction on the wing is lift.

## How the force reaches the wing: pressure

Air can only push on the wing through pressure, so the same thing can be described in pressure terms:

- **Over the top:** Air follows the curved upper surface. To bend around that curve, the air's pressure must be *lower* than the surrounding atmosphere. This low pressure also speeds the air up (Bernoulli's principle). The air over the top moves considerably faster than the air below.
- **Underneath:** The tilted lower surface slows and slightly compresses the oncoming air, so pressure there is a bit *higher*.

Lower pressure on top and higher pressure below create a net upward force. Typically the suction on the upper surface contributes the larger share.

The Newton picture (air pushed down) and the Bernoulli picture (pressure difference) aren't competing theories. They're two descriptions of the same flow.

## A common myth

You may have heard that air over the top must go faster because it has "farther to travel" and must meet the bottom air at the trailing edge at the same time (the **equal transit time** idea). This is false. The two parcels don't meet up, and the top air actually arrives at the trailing edge *well before* the bottom air. The explanation also can't account for flat wings, paper airplanes, or planes flying upside down, all of which generate lift.

## What controls how much lift you get

Lift ≈ ½ × air density × velocity² × wing area × lift coefficient

- **Speed:** Doubling speed gives about four times the lift.
- **Angle of attack:** Tilting the wing up more increases lift, but only up to a point. Beyond roughly 15°, the airflow separates from the upper surface, lift collapses, and the wing **stalls**.
- **Air density:** Thinner air (high altitude, hot days) gives less lift, so planes need longer runways in Denver in summer.
- **Wing shape and area:** Flaps and slats extend during takeoff and landing to increase camber and area, giving more lift at low speeds.

**In short:** the wing's shape and tilt bend the airflow downward, creating low pressure above and higher pressure below, and that pressure difference holds the plane up.
