# How does an airplane wing make lift?

_v0.6_

A wing makes lift by **turning the oncoming air downward**. To turn the air, the wing must create a pressure difference, and that pressure difference pushes the wing up.

1. **The wing deflects the flow.** The wing meets the air at a small angle (the *angle of attack*, typically 2–10°), and its upper surface curves. Air that flows over and under the wing leaves the trailing edge moving downward.
2. **Curved flow requires a pressure difference.** Air moves in a curved path only when a pressure difference pushes it sideways, toward the inside of the curve. Above the wing, the air curves down to follow the surface, so the pressure there falls below atmospheric pressure. Below the wing, the air is slowed and pushed down, so the pressure there rises above atmospheric pressure.
3. **The pressure difference lifts the wing.** Lower pressure on top and higher pressure below give a net upward force. By Newton's third law, this is the same force that pushes the air down. "Pressure difference" and "air pushed down" are two descriptions of one mechanism.

**Example.** A Cessna 172 weighs about 1,100 kg and has 16.2 m² of wing. Lift must equal weight: 1,100 × 9.81 ≈ 10,800 N. Divide by the wing area, and the average pressure difference is about **670 Pa**. That is only about 0.7% of atmospheric pressure (101,325 Pa). A small pressure difference over a large area carries the aircraft.

**A common error:** the "equal transit time" story says that air on top must travel farther, so it goes faster to rejoin the lower air. This is false. Air over the top actually arrives at the trailing edge first. The air on top moves faster *because* the pressure there is low (Bernoulli's principle), not because of a meeting requirement.
