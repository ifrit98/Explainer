# Why does ice float on water?

_none_

Ice floats because it's **less dense than liquid water**, about 0.92 g/cm³ versus about 1.00 g/cm³. That's unusual, since most substances are denser as solids than as liquids.

### Why ice is less dense: hydrogen bonding

1. **Water molecules are polar.** Each H₂O molecule has a slightly negative oxygen end and two slightly positive hydrogen ends. This lets molecules attract each other through **hydrogen bonds**.

2. **In liquid water**, hydrogen bonds constantly form, break, and re-form. Molecules tumble around and can pack fairly closely together.

3. **When water freezes**, each molecule locks into hydrogen bonds with four neighbors in a fixed tetrahedral arrangement. This forms an open, **hexagonal crystal lattice** with a lot of empty space in it.

4. **That open structure takes up more volume** than the same number of molecules in the liquid. So water expands by about 9% when it freezes.

### Buoyancy does the rest

By Archimedes' principle, an object floats if it's less dense than the fluid it's in. Since ice is about 92% as dense as water, roughly **90% of floating ice sits below the surface**. That's where the phrase "tip of the iceberg" comes from.

### Why it matters

- **Lakes freeze from the top down.** The ice layer insulates the water below, so aquatic life can survive winter.
- **Freeze-thaw weathering.** Water expanding in rock cracks breaks rocks apart over time.
- **Burst pipes.** Freezing water expands with enough force to crack metal.

A related quirk: liquid water is densest at about **4 °C**. Below that temperature, molecules begin forming the open, ice-like arrangement even before freezing.
