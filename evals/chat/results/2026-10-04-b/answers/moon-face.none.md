# Why do we always see the same side of the Moon?

_none_

We always see the same side of the Moon because of **tidal locking**: the Moon rotates on its axis exactly once for every orbit it makes around Earth, about every 27.3 days. Since its spin and orbit are in sync, the same hemisphere always faces us.

## How it happened

1. **Earth's gravity stretched the Moon.** Gravity weakens with distance, so Earth pulls harder on the Moon's near side than its far side. This stretched the Moon slightly into an oblong shape, with tidal bulges along the Earth–Moon line.

2. **The early Moon spun faster.** Billions of years ago, the Moon rotated more quickly than it orbited. Its rotation kept carrying the bulges out of alignment with Earth.

3. **Earth's gravity acted as a brake.** Earth kept tugging the misaligned bulges back toward itself. This created a torque that opposed the Moon's spin, and internal friction from the constant flexing turned rotational energy into heat.

4. **The spin settled into sync.** The Moon's rotation slowed until it matched its orbital period. At that point the bulges stay pointed at Earth, there's no more net torque, and the arrangement is stable.

## Some nuances

- **We actually see about 59% of the surface.** The Moon's orbit is slightly elliptical and tilted, so it appears to rock back and forth a little, an effect called *libration*. Over time this lets us peek a bit around the edges.
- **The "far side" isn't the "dark side."** The far side gets just as much sunlight as the near side. It's only "dark" in the sense that it was unknown until the Soviet Luna 3 probe photographed it in 1959.
- **It's common in the solar system.** Most large moons are tidally locked to their planets, including Jupiter's Galilean moons and Saturn's Titan. Pluto and its moon Charon are *mutually* locked, each always showing the same face to the other.
- **Earth is slowly being affected too.** The Moon's tides are gradually slowing Earth's rotation, lengthening our day by about 2 milliseconds per century. The Moon is also drifting away by roughly 3.8 cm per year.
