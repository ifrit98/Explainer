# Why does salt melt ice on roads?

_v0.6_

Salt does not heat the ice. It lowers the temperature at which water freezes, so ice at −5 °C that was stable becomes unstable and melts.

**The mechanism.** At the surface of ice, two processes run at the same time:

- Water molecules leave the solid and join the liquid (melting).
- Liquid molecules lock onto the solid (freezing).

At 0 °C in pure water, the two rates are equal, so nothing changes. That balance is the freezing point.

Salt needs a little liquid to start. Ice almost always has a thin liquid film, or it gets one from sun, traffic, or the brine that road crews spray. Salt dissolves in that film and splits into Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. The ions dilute the water, so fewer water molecules at the boundary are in position to lock onto the ice. The freezing rate drops, but the melting rate does not change. Melting wins, and the film grows and dissolves more salt.

Melting absorbs heat, which comes from the road and the air. The system settles at a new, lower temperature where the two rates match again.

**With numbers.** The freezing point drops by about 1.86 °C for each mole of dissolved particles per kilogram of water. Dissolve 58 g of salt (1 mol NaCl) in 1 kg of water. It gives 2 mol of ions, so the brine freezes at about 2 × 1.86 ≈ −3.7 °C. More salt gives a lower freezing point, down to a floor of about −21 °C.

**The consequence.** The effect depends only on how many particles dissolve, not on what they are. Calcium chloride splits into 3 ions instead of 2, so it works at colder temperatures. In practice, rock salt becomes slow below about −10 °C, and crews switch to calcium chloride or use sand for grip instead.
