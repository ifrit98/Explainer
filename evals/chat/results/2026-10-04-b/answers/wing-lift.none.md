# How does an airplane wing make lift?

_none_

An airplane wing makes lift by **deflecting air downward**. By Newton's third law, pushing air down pushes the wing up. The wing's shape and angle create a pressure difference: lower pressure above the wing, higher pressure below. That pressure difference is the force we call lift.

## How it works

**1. Angle of attack**
A wing meets the oncoming air tilted slightly upward, at what's called the angle of attack. This alone forces air downward off the bottom surface and makes the air flowing over the top follow a downward-curving path.

**2. Airfoil shape (camber)**
Most wings are curved, usually more on top than on the bottom. This helps the airflow turn smoothly downward and lets the wing produce lift efficiently, even at small angles.

**3. Flow turning creates pressure differences**
To make air follow a curved path, there must be a pressure gradient across the flow:
- **Above the wing**, the air curves around the upper surface, and the pressure there drops below the surrounding atmospheric pressure. This suction usually provides most of the lift.
- **Below the wing**, air is slowed and pushed, raising the pressure slightly.

**4. Speed and Bernoulli**
Air over the top of the wing really does move faster, and Bernoulli's principle correctly links that faster flow with lower pressure. Bernoulli and Newton aren't competing explanations. They are two views of the same airflow.

## A common myth: "equal transit time"

Many textbooks claim that air over the top must travel farther and so must "catch up" with the air underneath at the trailing edge. **This is false.** Nothing requires the two parcels of air to meet again. In fact, the air over the top arrives at the trailing edge *well before* the air underneath. The speed difference is real, but that reasoning for it is wrong.

## What affects how much lift you get

The lift equation summarizes it:

**L = ½ · ρ · v² · S · C_L**

- **ρ (air density):** thinner air at high altitude gives less lift.
- **v (airspeed):** lift grows with the *square* of speed, so doubling speed quadruples lift.
- **S (wing area):** bigger wings give more lift.
- **C_L (lift coefficient):** depends on the wing's shape and angle of attack.

## Stalls

Increasing the angle of attack increases lift, but only up to a point, typically around 15° for many wings. Beyond that, air can no longer follow the curved upper surface. It separates into turbulent eddies, and lift drops sharply. This is a **stall**. It's about angle, not speed, though flying slowly forces pilots to use higher angles, which brings them closer to it.

**In short:** the wing is a device for turning airflow downward. That turning creates low pressure above and higher pressure below, and the net result is an upward force.
