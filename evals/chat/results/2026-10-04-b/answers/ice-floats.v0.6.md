# Why does ice float on water?

_v0.6_

Ice floats because it is **less dense than liquid water**. A floating object sinks until it pushes aside its own weight of water. Ice reaches that point before it is fully under the surface.

**Why ice is less dense.** Most substances shrink when they freeze, because the molecules pack more tightly in a solid. Water does the opposite because of **hydrogen bonds**. A hydrogen bond is a weak attraction between the slightly positive hydrogen of one water molecule and the slightly negative oxygen of another.

- **In ice**, each molecule holds exactly four neighbours by hydrogen bonds, in a fixed tetrahedral geometry. This geometry builds an open hexagonal lattice with a lot of empty space inside it.
- **In liquid water**, the hydrogen bonds break and re-form continuously. The lattice collapses, and molecules move into the gaps. So the same number of molecules fits into a smaller volume.

**The numbers.**
- Ice at 0 °C: 0.917 g/cm³
- Liquid water at 0 °C: 1.000 g/cm³

So 1 kg of water takes 1.00 L as a liquid and 1.09 L as ice. That is about 9% expansion.

**How much of the ice floats above the surface.** The submerged fraction equals the density ratio:

- In fresh water: 0.917 / 1.000 = 91.7% under the surface, 8.3% above.
- In seawater (1.025 g/cm³): 0.917 / 1.025 ≈ 89% under, 11% above.

This is why most of an iceberg is hidden.

**Why it matters.** Lakes freeze from the top down. The surface ice insulates the liquid water below it, so fish survive the winter.
