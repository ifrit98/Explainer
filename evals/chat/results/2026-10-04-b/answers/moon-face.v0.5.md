# Why we always see the same side of the Moon

**Short answer:** The Moon does spin. It turns once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to orbit Earth once, about 27.3 days. Earth's tides forced this match long ago. The match is called **tidal locking**.

## 1. The Moon spins, at a matched rate

Many people think the Moon does not rotate. It does, and here is why it must.

Put a marker on the Moon's near side, pointing at Earth.

- **If the Moon did not spin:** after half an orbit, the Moon is on the other side of Earth. The marker still points the same direction in space, which is now *away* from Earth. Over one month, we would see every side.
- **If the Moon spins once per orbit:** the marker turns by half a turn while the Moon moves halfway around. It still points at Earth. This is what we observe.

A useful comparison: walk around a chair while you keep your face toward it. You also turn once around yourself on every lap.

## 2. How Earth forced the match

The cause is **tidal locking** (an established fact; the early details are estimates).

1. **Gravity stretches the Moon.** Earth pulls harder on the Moon's near side than on its far side. This stretches the Moon a little along the Earth–Moon line. The result is two small **bulges**, one facing Earth and one facing away.
2. **A faster spin drags the bulges off the line.** The young Moon probably spun faster than it orbited. Its rock cannot reshape instantly, so the spin carried the bulges slightly ahead of the Earth–Moon line.
3. **Earth pulls the bulges back.** Earth's gravity pulls on the off-line bulge. This creates a **torque**, a twisting force, that opposes the spin.
4. **Friction removes the spin energy.** The rock flexes on every turn, and the flexing turns spin energy into heat. The spin slows.
5. **The process stops at a match.** When spin period equals orbital period, the bulges stay on the Earth–Moon line. Earth's pull no longer causes a torque, so the spin stays at that rate.

This happened early in the Moon's history, probably within its first few hundred million years at most (estimate). It happened to the Moon first because Earth's tidal effect on the Moon is about 20 times stronger than the Moon's effect on Earth. Earth is 81 times more massive, and the Moon is only 0.27 times Earth's radius.

## 3. Common points of confusion

- **"Same side" is not exact.** We see about 59% of the surface over time, not 50%. The orbit is an ellipse, so the Moon's orbital speed changes but its spin rate does not. The Moon's axis also tilts slightly relative to its orbit. These two effects make it appear to rock back and forth. This rocking is called **libration**.
- **The far side is not always dark.** It gets as much sunlight as the near side. At new Moon, the far side is fully lit.
- **Locking is common.** Most large moons in the Solar System are tidally locked to their planets. Pluto and its moon Charon are locked to *each other*: each always shows the same face to the other.

## 4. The process continues on Earth

The same mechanism acts on Earth in reverse, but more slowly.

- The Moon raises bulges in Earth's oceans and rock. Earth spins faster than the Moon orbits, so these bulges run ahead and are pulled back. This slows Earth's rotation, and days get longer by about 2 milliseconds per century (measurement).
- The torque also pushes the Moon outward. Laser ranging measures the Moon moving away about 3.8 cm per year (observation).
- Given enough time, Earth would also lock and show one face to the Moon. That would take far longer than the Sun's remaining lifetime (model output).
