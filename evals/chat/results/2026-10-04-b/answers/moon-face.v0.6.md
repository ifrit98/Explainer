# Why do we always see the same side of the Moon?

_v0.6_

We always see the same side because the Moon spins exactly once on its axis in the time it takes to orbit Earth once: 27.3 days for each. This match is called **tidal locking**.

The Moon does rotate. If it did not, a fixed crater would point toward Earth at one point in the orbit and away from Earth half an orbit later. Over a month we would see the whole surface. Because one spin takes exactly one orbit, the same face stays turned toward us.

**Why the two periods match**

1. Earth's gravity pulls harder on the Moon's near side than on its far side. This difference stretches the Moon slightly along the Earth–Moon line into two bulges.
2. Suppose the Moon once spun faster than it orbited. Its rotation would carry the bulges ahead of the Earth–Moon line.
3. Earth's gravity pulls the leading bulge back toward that line. This pull is a **torque**, a twisting force, and it slows the spin.
4. The slowing stops only when the bulges no longer move relative to Earth. That happens when one spin equals one orbit.

A body that started spinning too slowly would be sped up by the same torque. Either way, the stable end state is a locked spin.

The Moon locked early, probably within its first hundred million years or less (estimate). It locked before Earth did because Earth's mass is about 81 times the Moon's. Earth therefore raises much larger tides on the Moon than the Moon raises on Earth. The Moon also had far less spin to lose.

Because the Moon's orbit is slightly elliptical and tilted, it appears to wobble a little as seen from Earth. This wobble lets us see about 59% of its surface over time, not exactly 50%.
