# Why does salt melt ice on roads?

_none_

Salt melts ice by **lowering the freezing point of water**, a phenomenon called **freezing point depression**.

### What's happening at the molecular level

1. **Ice is always partly melting and refreezing.** Even below 0°C, the surface of ice has a thin film of liquid water. Molecules constantly leave the solid ice for that liquid layer and rejoin it. At 0°C, these two rates are balanced.

2. **Salt dissolves into that liquid layer.** Sodium chloride (NaCl) breaks apart into sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) ions in the water film.

3. **The ions get in the way of refreezing.** Dissolved ions interfere with water molecules trying to lock back into the ice crystal. Melting continues at the same rate, but refreezing slows down, so more ice melts than forms.

4. **The result is brine.** Salty water has a lower freezing point than pure water. Water would need to get colder than 0°C to freeze again.

### Why it's a colligative property

The effect depends on the **number of dissolved particles**, not what they are. Because NaCl splits into two ions, it is about twice as effective per molecule as a substance that doesn't dissociate. Calcium chloride (CaCl₂) splits into three ions, which is one reason it works even better.

### The limits

- **Rock salt stops working well below about −10°C (15°F)**, and it becomes largely ineffective near −18°C (0°F). Saturated salt water freezes around −21°C (−6°F).
- In very cold conditions, road crews switch to **calcium chloride** or **magnesium chloride**, which work at much lower temperatures. They also release heat when they dissolve, which helps jump-start melting.
- Salt needs some liquid water to start working, which is why crews often spray **brine** before a storm. Traffic also helps by grinding the salt in.

### Bonus: the same principle elsewhere

- Salt is added to ice in **homemade ice cream makers** to make the ice-water mix colder than 0°C, so it can freeze the cream.
- The ocean freezes at about −2°C because of its salt content.
